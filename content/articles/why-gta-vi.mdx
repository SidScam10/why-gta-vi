Anyone who's flying into Miami this week, look out of the window on the way down. On the roof of the basketball arena downtown, in neon pink letters big enough to read from a plane, it says "Welcome to Vice City". Vice City does not exist. It is the fictional Miami of the *Grand Theft Auto* games, and Rockstar Games, the studio that makes them, paid the county close to a million dollars to put that name on the skyline.

On ==Thursday, 19 November==, *Grand Theft Auto VI* comes out. I probably have been waiting for it close to thirteen years now. And I think it is ==the biggest piece of gaming technology and entertainment== to be released in a decade, and nothing will to come close to it for the next ten or fifteen years.

My history with the Grand Theft Auto series is well-documented. Anyone who knows me is acutely aware of just how much I yap about it. It all started back in 2010, when I asked, "Can I try that car game that's clearly not meant for someone my age?". Luckily, I had a cool uncle who let me loose in GTA IV’s *Liberty City*, where I spent my time launching pedestrians into orbit and laughing the whole way.

I know how that sounds if you don't play games. You may know *GTA* only as the one where you steal cars. So before I make my case, look at how people with no interest in my excitement have behaved. Other publishers cleared out of November. Microsoft moved its big fantasy game, *Fable*, to February. The makers of *Squadron 42* pushed their release into the middle of next year and said plainly that *GTA VI* was the reason. A US Army unit in Georgia offered its soldiers a four-day pass for launch week. When Rockstar showed 26 minutes of the game running in August, the video premiered on Netflix, which no game had done before, and both Netflix and the streaming site Twitch struggled under the traffic. The research firm Newzoo says the entire console business would shrink this year if this one game were not in it.

I am writing this for people who build software, who like knowing how things are made, and who have never understood why people lose their minds over a video game. I am not trying to turn you into a gamer. I want to show you what is underneath the noise: a studio with a 25-year habit of resetting what the medium can do, given more time, money and people than anyone else in its field, shipping a world that thousands of people built almost entirely by hand. I will also tell you what it cost some of them, because that belongs in the story too.

<Chapter title="THE MEDIUM MOVED" subtitle="Games got bigger than film and music while most people weren't watching" art="/img/why-gta-vi/ch-1.jpg" />

Recorded music, all of it, worldwide, earned about $32 billion last year. Cinema box offices took roughly the same. Video games earned $189 billion, around three times the other two put together, and the forecasts for this year pass $210 billion.

The comparison holds when you put one game next to one film. *Avengers: Endgame*, the biggest opening in cinema history, took about $1.2 billion worldwide in its first weekend. *Grand Theft Auto V* took a billion dollars in three days in 2013, faster than any entertainment release before it. Then it did something no film does. It kept selling for thirteen years, across three generations of hardware, to nearly 235 million copies. Only *Minecraft* has sold more.

<PullQuote cite="How I'd Explain the GTA 6 Hype to a Normie">A movie happens to you, while you happen to a game.</PullQuote>

That line, from a YouTube video I keep sending to friends, is the best explanation I have found for why the money works this way. A film is two and a half hours and it is the same two and a half hours for everyone in the room. *GTA* is what is called an ==open-world game==: one large, continuous place you can cross in any direction, in any order, instead of a sequence of levels. People spend hundreds of hours inside it, and no two of them spend those hours the same way.

<YouTube url="https://youtu.be/JQEulR8gOsE?si=kdmX6I9P5EVIoK21" title="How I'd Explain the GTA 6 Hype to a Normie" caption="The video the quote comes from. If you have twenty minutes, it covers the cultural side better than I can." />

If you are an engineer, the hardware curve is the quickest way to see how far this has come. In 1985, all of *Super Mario Bros.* fit into about 40 kilobytes. In 2001, *Grand Theft Auto III* ran a full 3D city on a PlayStation 2 with 32 megabytes of RAM, pulling the streets off a spinning disc quickly enough that you could drive from one end to the other without a loading screen. The PlayStation 5 that *GTA VI* targets has 16 gigabytes, 500 times as much, along with a solid-state drive and a graphics chip built for ray tracing, which I'll come back to.

More hardware has never made the job easier, because all of it gets spent. A game draws the world as a series of still pictures, called frames. At 30 frames per second, the machine has ==33 milliseconds== to read your input, move every car and pedestrian, run the physics, pull new scenery off the drive and draw the picture. Then it has to do it again. If it runs late, you see the stutter. Everything I describe later in this piece happens inside that 33-millisecond window.

One studio has spent 25 years being better at that problem than everyone else. I think of Rockstar the way I think of Google or Microsoft at their peak: the place the best people in the field want to work, and the one whose releases move the baseline for everybody else. Each generation of consoles has had its technical high point set by a Rockstar game, from *GTA III* on the PlayStation 2 to *Red Dead Redemption 2* on the PlayStation 4.

That record buys a kind of trust I have not seen anywhere else in entertainment. When the standard price for a big game was $70, Rockstar priced this one at $80, with a $100 edition above it. People complained, me included. Then, by one estimate, nine out of ten people who pre-ordered chose the $100 one. My own reasoning lasted about four seconds: "It's ₹7.5k, but it's Rockstar."

<Chapter title="BUILT ON A BUG" subtitle="A short history of the studio, starting with a defect nobody fixed" art="/img/why-gta-vi/ch-2.jpg" />

Most engineers have a story about a bug that turned into a feature. Rockstar has one that turned into a company.

In 1995, a small studio in Dundee, Scotland, called DMA Design started work on a top-down driving game about cops and robbers. It was called *Race'n'Chase*, it ran more than a year late, and by the accounts of the people who made it, it was unstable and dull. It came close to being cancelled. Then a fault in the pathfinding broke the police cars. In the words of Gary Penn, who worked on it, "the police suddenly became mental and aggressive." They stopped trying to pull the player over and started trying to drive straight through them.

The testers ignored the missions and spent their time seeing how long they could survive. The team kept the bug, rebuilt the game around it, and released it in 1997 under a new name: *Grand Theft Auto*.

A year later, two brothers from London, Sam and Dan Houser, moved to New York and founded Rockstar Games around the series. The Dundee studio moved to Edinburgh and became Rockstar North, which still leads development today. Sam Houser is still Rockstar's president. Dan, who wrote every main *GTA* for two decades, left in 2020, and *GTA VI* is the first one written without him.

<YouTube url="https://youtu.be/poKPUS7pu5Y?si=aNAJnd3HjvuG9jGK" title="History of Grand Theft Auto (1984-2021)" caption="A longer history of the early games, for anyone who wants it." />

The game that made the reputation was *GTA III* in 2001. It moved the series into three dimensions and gave you a city you could simply be in. Aaron Garbut, who led the art on that game and is one of the three people running *GTA VI*, has said the aim from the start was a place where players would be happy just to exist. Two more games followed quickly on the same foundations, and by 2004 the series was the biggest thing in the medium.

<PullQuote cite="Sam Houser, 2005">A truly realised, truly immersive, living, breathing world.</PullQuote>

That sentence is the nearest thing Rockstar has to a mission statement, and every release since has been an attempt to get closer to it. *GTA IV* in 2008 moved to an engine Rockstar had built for itself, and gave its characters bodies that reacted to being hit instead of playing a canned animation. *GTA V* in 2013 came out at the very end of a console generation and looked like it belonged to the next one. *Red Dead Redemption 2*, a western released in 2018, took around eight years and two thousand people.

My favourite fact about that last game is a small one. In cold weather, a horse's more *delicate* features visibly react to the temperature. According to Jason Schreier, the *Bloomberg* reporter who has covered Rockstar for years, nobody in charge asked for it. Somebody on the team decided the world would be more real with it and put it in. I think of that detail whenever someone asks me what is different about this studio. It is a chase for perfection that borders on the ridiculous, and it runs all the way down to individual people.

The series has always been built as a satire of America: its radio adverts, its politics, its television, and now its influencers. The uncomfortable part is how the satire ages, because real life keeps catching up with it. It has also been in trouble almost continuously. *GTA III* started a decade of arguments about violent games. In 2005, modders (fans who alter a game's files) found a disabled sex scene left in the code of *San Andreas* and switched it back on. The game was re-rated for adults only, shops pulled it from the shelves, and Senator Hillary Clinton called for a federal investigation. Dead code, shipped to production, became a national political incident. Later games drew protests over drunk-driving, over an interactive torture scene and over how they portrayed gay characters. And in 2021 Rockstar embarrassed itself without anyone's help, releasing a remastered collection of its old games in a badly broken state.

Plot Rockstar's releases on a timeline and the shape is hard to miss. Through the 2000s there is a new game almost every year, sometimes several. After 2013 the line goes nearly empty.

*GTA VI* has ==eleven times the animation== of *GTA V*. Of the characters those animations belong to, Garbut told *The New York Times*: "We art-directed each individual one of them." To get there, Rockstar roughly doubled in size after *Red Dead Redemption 2*.

Nobody else in the industry gets to work like this. Rockstar doesn't publish its budgets, but outside estimates for *GTA VI* start at a billion dollars and go up, which would make it the most expensive game ever made. The patience was affordable for a simple reason: *GTA V* never stopped earning. With that money coming in every quarter, nobody ever had to ship this game before it was ready.

<Chapter title="33 MILLISECONDS" subtitle="What the machine is doing, as far as anyone outside can tell" art="/img/why-gta-vi/ch-4.jpg" />

A brief warning before the technical part - Rockstar says almost nothing in public about how its software works. What follows comes from people who are very good at reading footage: the visual-effects artists at Corridor Crew, who spent a video taking the August gameplay apart shot by shot, and the hardware analysts at Digital Foundry. It also draws on patents filed by Rockstar's parent company, Take-Two. Treat it as informed analysis and educated guesses. It is not official documentation.

<YouTube url="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ULM2OIj5o0I" title="VFX Artists Breakdown GTA 6's IMPOSSIBLE Graphics" caption="Corridor Crew's breakdown. Most of the rendering explanations in this chapter start here." />

Start with the engine. A game engine is the runtime and toolchain underneath a game: the renderer, the physics, the animation system, the audio, the code that streams the world off the drive, and the editors the artists work in all day. Most studios license one, the way most tech companies build on *AWS* now. Rockstar did too, until 2004, when the company that made its engine, *RenderWare*, was bought by Electronic Arts, a direct rival. Rockstar's response was to build its own, called Rockstar Advanced Game Engine - RAGE for short, and they has used it for every major game since.

You will recognise the trade-off. A general-purpose engine has to serve thousands of customers making every kind of game. RAGE has one customer, and for twenty years that customer has poured its effort into the same few problems: streaming a dense city off a disc, filling it with people, and lighting it at every hour of the day.

### Bodies that try to stay upright

In most games a character is a state machine. Walking, running and turning are recorded clips. Hit the character hard enough and a trigger flips them into "ragdoll", a limp skeleton that drops like a mannequin. It looks as fake as it sounds.

Rockstar uses a system called Euphoria, owned by their parent company Take-Two Interactive, first seen in *GTA IV*, that the Corridor artists describe as a simulated nervous system. When a character is hit, the system looks at where their weight is, where their feet are and how hard the blow was, and works out what a body would try to do about it: shift to the other leg, stumble, and if the balance is gone, get the arms up to protect the head. The recorded animation and the physics run together and pass control between them. Every character on screen is running a small feedback controller, every frame.

<YouTube url="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HauN98naZ9U" title="NaturalMotion Euphoria demonstration" caption="The original demonstration from NaturalMotion, the Oxford company that created Euphoria: ordinary ragdoll on one side, a body that tries to save itself on the other." />

The part I enjoy most is that the film industry's version of this tool is no longer sold. The Corridor artists, who do VFX for a living, end their video asking Rockstar to release its copy. Professionals from film are jealous of a game studio's tooling.

### Reflections, and what it costs to do them properly

To draw a reflection in a window, most games cheat. They take pixels that are already on the screen and flip them. It costs almost nothing, and it has one flaw you can't unsee once you've noticed it: anything that is not on screen can't be reflected, so reflections vanish as you tilt the camera.

The honest way is ray tracing. For each shiny pixel you fire a ray from the camera, bounce it, find out what it hits in the scene and draw that, even if the thing it hits is behind you. Film does this as a matter of course, and a single film frame can take hours to render. To do it in ==33 milliseconds== you fire very few rays, accept a noisy, blotchy result, and clean it up with a filter that borrows information from the frames before. Sample sparsely, reconstruct, and spread the cost over time.

In the August footage there is a glass door that reflects a woman at a bar behind the camera, which is only possible with ray tracing. On a table nearby, a cocktail glass bends the image of the place mat behind it, which means each ray is passing through four surfaces of glass.

<Figure src="/img/why-gta-vi/reflections.jpg" alt="The glass door and the cocktail glass, from Rockstar's Extended Look. Captured on a PlayStation 5." caption="The glass door and the cocktail glass, from Rockstar's Extended Look. Captured on a PlayStation 5." wide />

What convinced me, though, was what they left out. A vase further back on the same table has no refraction at all. Fire is still drawn as flat animated images. Water in a fountain doesn't splash. Somebody decided, object by object, where the expensive effect would be noticed and where it wouldn't. That is a budget being managed by people who know exactly what a millisecond is worth.

### Light that bounces

In a real room, light hits a wall, loses some energy and bounces onto everything else. Every surface is a dim lamp. This is called global illumination, and not having it is the main reason older games look flat next to film.

Calculating it in full is far beyond a console. What the Corridor artists see in *GTA VI* is a grid laid over the world, with a "light probe" in every cell. Think of each one as a tiny 360-degree camera taking a quick, low-resolution picture of the light arriving at that spot. Characters and objects are lit by the probes nearest to them. The probes update every frame, and each update builds on the one before, so the lighting converges over a handful of frames, with each pass adding another bounce.

If you work with distributed systems you already know this pattern. It is an iterative solver spread across time, or a cache that is always a few frames stale and always catching up.

<Figure src="/img/why-gta-vi/lighting.jpg" alt="Bounced light inside a car, with no hand-placed lamps. From Rockstar's Extended Look." caption="Bounced light inside a car, with no hand-placed lamps. From Rockstar's Extended Look." wide />

It also solves a production problem that has no manual answer. On a film set, someone lights each shot. In an open world, the player can drive any car down any street at any hour in any weather, and nobody can light all of that by hand. A system has to. Digital Foundry's reading is that *GTA VI* relies on ==ray-traced lighting== throughout, with no older fallback underneath it.

The price is on the box. The game runs at 30 frames per second, and the August footage was rendered at roughly 1440p and scaled up to 4K. The analysts don't expect a 60-frame mode on today's consoles. I'll take that trade.

### Hair, sweat, and the things nobody asked for

Most games draw hair as a few hundred flat ribbons with a hair pattern painted on. Film simulates a few guide strands and fills in thousands of hairs between them. *GTA VI* appears to do it the film way for every character, including the people in the background. In one shot a woman behind the main action flicks her hair and it catches on her shoulder, because her shoulder is sweaty. Digital Foundry called it the best hair they have seen in a game.

<Figure src="/img/why-gta-vi/sweat.jpg" alt="Characters sweating while dancing at a nightclub" caption="Characters sweating while dancing at a nightclub" wide />

The sweat matches one of Take-Two's patents, for simulating liquid on a surface without simulating real liquid. A small, flat fluid simulation is wrapped onto the skin, so sweat beads, runs and collects. According to Schreier, characters in the game's nightclubs sweat more the harder they dance.

### What the patents say

Game studios rarely bother with patents. Take-Two holds more than ten that various forum threads have linked to this game, and they read like a syllabus.

| Patent | What it describes |
|---|---|
| US11620781B1 | Building character movement from small reusable blocks, combined as the game runs |
| US11684855B2 | A road-network graph for routing large numbers of cars and people |
| US10685152B2 | Liquid on surfaces: sweat, rain, dirt |
| US11978162B2 | Models that gain and shed detail smoothly with distance |


The first one matters most. Instead of recording a separate animation for every situation a character could be in, movement is assembled on the fly from small pieces, so a person can adapt to a slope or a tight corner that nobody recorded.

All of this exists for one reason. A film only builds what the camera will see, and a car in the background of a shot is a prop moved from A to B. A game has no fixed camera. Every car, pedestrian, traffic light and rainstorm has to be a system that runs by itself and looks right from any angle at any time. When those systems meet, things happen that nobody wrote. Phil Hooker, another of the three people running the project, described sitting down to test a story mission and still not having reached it two hours later. A minor collision became a fight, the fight became a robbery, the robbery became a police chase, and he ended up following a stranger's car to a compound he hadn't known was there.

<YouTube url="https://youtu.be/tJbzMqJGH4k" title="Grand Theft Auto VI: An Extended Look" caption="Rockstar's 26 minutes of gameplay, captured on a PlayStation 5. Everything in this chapter can be seen in it." />

<Chapter title="HAND-CRAFTED SAUCE" subtitle="Every room, every face, and no generative AI" art="/img/why-gta-vi/ch-5.jpg" />

The systems are half of it. The other half is the part that takes eight years.

Garbut has described how the interiors were made. For every room in the game, somebody decided who sits where, what they are drinking and what marks are left on the table. The photographs on the wall show the people who live there. Rockstar's staff have been travelling to Miami for research since 2015. The studio commissioned more than fifty street artists to paint the game's murals, and brought in a Miami stylist to get the clothes right. When it became clear that filling the streets with believable people was more work than the company could absorb, Rockstar set up a studio in Los Angeles, staffed with writers, directors and producers, that works on nothing but NPCs. (NPC stands for non-player character: every person in the world who isn't controlled by the player.)

<PullQuote cite="Aaron Garbut">There's no one person that has this whole thing in their head.</PullQuote>

That is the sentence I would put in front of any engineer. This is a system too large for any one person to understand, built by thousands of people over most of a decade, some of whom have worked on nothing else since 2015. It still has to feel like one thing made by one mind.

To see what that means, stop on a single frame.

<FullBleed src="/img/why-gta-vi/gta6-vice-city-skyline.jpg" alt="Daytime view of the Vice City skyline across the bay, with traffic on a bridge, a beach club, boats and an arena" caption="One frame of Vice City." credit="Rockstar Games" parallax />

This is my own breakdown, to show the kinds of work in one picture. Nobody leaked me an org chart.

1. **The city.** Streets and buildings modelled from a decade of reference photography, many with rooms you can walk into.
2. **Water, trees and sky.** Palms that move with the wind, a bay that reflects the skyline, weather that changes.
3. **The cars and boats.** Each one is an original design that parodies a real one, because *GTA* doesn't license real brands. Each has an interior and its own handling.
4. **Where everything is going.** The routing that keeps hundreds of cars and pedestrians moving sensibly.
5. **The people.** They now differ in height, build and age, which is far harder than it sounds, because a hand resting on a car door lands somewhere different on every body.
6. **How they move and what they say.** A share of those 600,000 animations, and the lines written for them in Los Angeles.
7. **Hair, skin and clothes.** Strands, sweat, fabric that wrinkles as someone walks.
8. **Every sign.** Billboards, shop fronts, invented brands and murals, which is where much of the satire lives.
9. **The light.** The probe grid, recalculating as the sun moves.
10. **The sound.** Street noise, radio stations, and an original score by the French duo Justice.
11. **The people you'll never notice.** Testers, tools programmers and build engineers, keeping a ten-year project compiling.

An ordinary ==open-world game== reuses a few dozen pedestrians and a few hundred animations, and most of its buildings are painted boxes. That is the difference. The Corridor artists called *GTA* "the *Avatar* of video games": the project with enough money and time to invent the technology it needs, which the rest of the industry then inherits.

### No generative AI

There is one more reason this game feels to me like the end of something.

Over the last two years, big games have started shipping with material made by generative AI, and saying so. Rockstar went the other way. In February, Take-Two's chief executive, Strauss Zelnick, said that "generative AI has zero part in what Rockstar Games is building." In August, Rob Nelson, the public face of *GTA VI* and the third of its three heads of development, was asked directly whether generative AI was used to make the game. He said no.

I want to be careful about how far that goes. Rockstar is not the only studio to say this; CDPR - the makers of *The Witcher* have made a similar pledge. Take-Two as a company is running hundreds of AI experiments to save time and money elsewhere. And the game is full of the older kind of AI, the ordinary code that tells a pedestrian how to cross a road, which has nothing to do with language models. Rockstar's own staff clearly see it as an open question for the future, because the union ballot I'll come to includes a say over AI policy.

For this game, though, ==the line held==, and that matters to me more than I expected. A game this size is thousands of people's working years, compressed onto a drive. When I walk into some back room in Vice City and see an in-game magazine left on a table, I want to know a real developer made the conscious decision on whether it should be there.

<Chapter title="THE HUMAN COST" subtitle="A hack, a locked office, 34 dismissals and a vote" art="/img/why-gta-vi/ch-6.png" />

I am a fan of what Rockstar makes. I also think the company has treated people badly at times, and an honest account has to include both perspectives.

Start with the secrecy. In September 2022, one of the most guarded companies in entertainment was broken into by a 17-year-old. He didn't use anything exotic. He phished his way into Rockstar's Slack, posted 90 clips of unfinished footage online and threatened to release the source code. Garbut later compared it to having someone go through your belongings. The leaks have not stopped since. The first trailer got out hours early. This August, an anonymous account by the name of CyberLeek, drip-fed the game's map and more than a dozen clips over nine days, while selling votes on which clip would come next -  all in the name of pumping and dumping a memecoin.

Rockstar's answer was to lock the doors. Since April 2024, everyone has had to be in the office five days a week. People working on *GTA VI* can't reach the tools from home at all, so if your child is sick, you take a day's leave. *Bloomberg* calls it the most divisive policy in the company. Management says it will look again after launch, and doesn't expect to change it.

Then there is crunch, the industry's word for long stretches of overtime before a deadline. *Red Dead Redemption 2* involved months of 60-hour weeks for many of its staff, and in 2018 a remark from Dan Houser about 100-hour weeks set off a public argument that Rockstar deserved to lose. To its credit, the company changed after that. Contractors became full-time staff, schedules became more flexible, and for *GTA VI* the leadership's stated position has been that the game would not be finished by crunching. They hired more people and gave time off in return for extra hours. Schreier says developers raise the subject far less than they used to. The head of publishing, Jennifer Kolbe, admits it has not been perfect, and other reporting this year has been harsher, with staff describing mandated overtime and, after the second delay, very low morale.

The sharpest conflict is still going on. In October 2025, Rockstar dismissed 34 people. The company says they shared confidential information in a public forum. Their union says they were organisers discussing working conditions in a private union chat, and calls it union busting. In filings to an employment tribunal in Glasgow, the workers claim that Rockstar had an informant in that chat for more than two years. Closing arguments are being heard this week, two days before the game comes out. There is no judgment yet.

At the same time, more than 1,600 Rockstar staff in the UK are voting on whether to recognise a union. The ballot opened on Thursday and closes on 26 November, with the launch in the middle of it. Kolbe told *Bloomberg*, "if they want a union, they will have a union."

So that is the studio I am asking you to admire. I don't have a way to make those two things fit together, and I would distrust anyone who said they did. The people who made this game deserved better than some of what they got. They also made something nobody else could have made.

<Chapter title="MISMANAGED OR DELIBERATE" subtitle="Thirteen years between games, eight of them spent on this one" art="/img/why-gta-vi/ch-7.png" />

If you manage engineers, you have been waiting to ask this. Was *GTA VI* a badly run project, or is this what a well-run project of this size looks like?

The calendar first. Early work began in 2014, and then almost the whole company was pulled onto *Red Dead Redemption 2* until 2018. Real production started after that, so the true figure is about eight years. In that time the plan was cut down from something far larger; under its code name, Project Americas, it once covered parts of both North and South America. Dan Houser left. The pandemic arrived. The first trailer, in December 2023, promised a 2025 release that the people in charge say they believed in. The date then slipped twice, by a year in total.

The case for mismanagement is easy to make. The scope had to be cut. Three people share the top job, and some staff told *Bloomberg* that the direction they got was slow and contradictory. A public date was missed twice. A teenager walked in through the chat tool, and the response alienated the workforce.

The case for the defence is the previous two chapters. ==Six hundred thousand animations== and a city dressed room by room have no shortcut that Rockstar was willing to take. And if a company decides to stop paying for its deadlines with people's evenings and weekends, it has to pay in months on the calendar. I would defend that trade to anyone. When the numbers showed the plan didn't fit, Rockstar hired, and built a studio. When the game wasn't ready, it moved the date, twice, in an industry full of games that shipped broken because nobody would. The people running it say the foundations laid this time mean the next game won't take eight years.

My honest answer is that it was probably both, and Thursday will decide which version gets remembered. But I keep coming back to how rare it is, in any kind of engineering, for a team to be given enough time. This is what it looks like when one is.

<Chapter title="THE HYPE" subtitle="Whether it's deserved, and what could still go wrong" art="/img/why-gta-vi/ch-8.jpg" />

To understand the noise, you have to know what *GTA V* turned into after it came out.

A few weeks after launch, Rockstar switched on *GTA Online*, a shared version of the city that players could live in together. It is still being updated thirteen years later. It is also where the money came from, since players can buy in-game cash with real cash, and I'll admit it became far too expensive to keep up with if you only played casually.

Then the players did something nobody at Rockstar planned. On fan-run servers they started to roleplay: logging in every day as a police officer, a paramedic, a mechanic, a lawyer or a small-time crook, and acting out that life with hundreds of others while an audience watched live. *GTA V* was the most-watched game on Twitch in 2021, and again in 2024, by which point it was eleven years old. Rockstar fought the software behind those servers at first. In 2023 it bought the team that made it. For a great many people, *GTA* is not a crime game any more. It is a place where they meet their friends, and it connects people from every walk of life.

Thirteen years of waiting turned that audience into detectives. People built whole YouTube careers on guessing when a trailer might appear. Fans stood outside Rockstar's Edinburgh office counting late-night pizza deliveries. When the marketing finally started this summer, it ran at a scale I have not seen a film attempt. *And I enjoyed every single second of it.*

<Figure src="/img/why-gta-vi/cover-art.jpg" alt="Grand Theft Auto VI's official cover art." caption="Grand Theft Auto VI's official cover art." />

The cover art was an event. So was the soundtrack, which arrives on launch day as a 34-track album. There are murals in Miami, Brooklyn and Hollywood, a blimp, and a convoy of real cars rebuilt to match the ones in the game. The Miami Heat are holding a Vice City night at their home game on Wednesday, the evening before release. Although not everyone in Miami is pleased about their county being tied to a game about crime.

Is the hype warranted? ==Yes.== It could not be higher unless Rockstar announced that the game cures cancer, and I still think it is earned. There is a 25-year record with barely a miss in it. There is the scale of what was built. And there is the footage, which is not a promise or a concept video. It is the game, running on a console you can buy today.

<YouTube url="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rz1B4B2yBZY" title="Grand Theft Auto VI Launch Trailer" caption="The launch trailer, released on 15 October." />

Will they deliver? Nobody outside the studio knows, and there are real reasons for doubt. It runs at 30 frames per second. It costs $80. The boxed copy has no disc in it, only a download code. There is no PC version, and the new online mode won't arrive until next year. It is the first *GTA* without Dan Houser's writing, steered by three people where there used to be one, and finished by a workforce that has had a hard couple of years.

Then there is the phrase "too big to fail". With pre-orders this large, the game has in one sense already succeeded before anyone has played it, and that is exactly the position from which big games fall on their faces. *Cyberpunk 2077* had millions of pre-orders in 2020 and launched in such a state that Sony pulled it from sale. Rockstar's own broken remaster is the nearer warning. And because this is *GTA*, a loud group of people will attack it this week whatever it turns out to be.

I've weighed all of that. I believe in my heart and soul that these are probably the only people who consistently deliver the absolute cream of the crop, and I am going to take my own sweet time getting lost in the world they so painstakingly made by hand.

So if you do fly into Miami this week and see that sign on the roof, you'll know what it is advertising. We are about to consume years and months of a person's art, thousands of people's in fact, made in the belief that it will change other people's lives for the better. ==I think it will change mine.==
